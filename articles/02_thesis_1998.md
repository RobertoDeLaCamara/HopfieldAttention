# 2. My 1998 Thesis: Hopfield for the Shortest Path Problem

> *In 1998, as a Telecommunications Engineering student at the University of Valladolid, I wrote my thesis on applying Hopfield Networks to the Shortest Path Problem (SPP). I didn't know it then, but that decision would shape my relationship with artificial intelligence for the next 25 years.*

## 1998: The Context

The neural network field was in its second "winter." Vapnik's 1995 "Support Vector Machines" paper had shifted interest toward kernels. Hopfield was considered a dead end — elegant, but impractical.

Still, there was something about Hopfield Networks that fascinated me: the idea that a dynamical system could **find solutions by optimizing an energy function**. You didn't need labeled data, you didn't need backpropagation. You defined the problem as an energy function, and the network found a solution.

## The Problem: Shortest Path

The Shortest Path Problem (SPP) is fundamental in telecommunication networks: given a graph with costs on its edges, find the lowest-cost route between two nodes. Dijkstra's algorithm (1959) solves it in $O(E + V \log V)$. So why use a neural network?

The point wasn't to beat Dijkstra on speed. It was to show that **combinatorial optimization problems could be encoded as energy functions** and solved through neural dynamics. The thesis wasn't practical — it was conceptual.

## Encoding the SPP as Energy

For the SPP, the neuron matrix $V[i][j]$ represents "the edge from node $i$ to node $j$ is active in the route." The energy function has to encode three constraints:

**Flow conservation at the source:**
$$E_{source} = A \cdot (\sum_j V[source][j] - \sum_k V[k][source] - 1)^2$$

The source must have exactly one more unit of outgoing flow than incoming.

**Flow conservation at the destination:**
$$E_{dest} = A \cdot (\sum_k V[k][dest] - \sum_j V[dest][j] - 1)^2$$

The destination must have exactly one more unit of incoming flow than outgoing.

**Conservation at intermediate nodes:**
$$E_{inter} = A \cdot \sum_{i \neq s, d} (\sum_j V[i][j] - \sum_k V[k][i])^2$$

At intermediate nodes, incoming flow must equal outgoing flow.

**Cost minimization:**
$$E_{cost} = B \cdot \sum_{i,j} C[i][j] \cdot V[i][j]$$

Where $C[i][j]$ is the cost of the edge.

Total energy is $E = E_{source} + E_{dest} + E_{inter} + E_{cost}$.

The network minimizes this energy through gradient descent: each neuron adjusts its activation to reduce the total energy.

## What Worked

The thesis showed that **the approach was viable**. For small graphs (< 20 nodes), the Hopfield Network found valid paths. The energy function converged, and visualizing the activation matrix showed interpretable patterns: the edges on the optimal route emerged as the most active.

## What Didn't Work

- **40-60% reliability**: The network didn't always converge to a valid solution. Local minima corresponded to invalid routes (cycles, incomplete paths).
- **Hyperparameter dependence**: The weights A and B needed manual tuning. Too little A → invalid routes. Too little B → long routes.
- **Scalability**: Graphs with more than 50 nodes collapsed into spurious solutions.
- **Time**: 5-10 seconds per query, versus milliseconds for Dijkstra.

## Why It Matters (25 Years Later)

In 1998, these problems looked like fatal limitations. But 25 years later, **every one of those limitations has a parallel in modern deep learning research**:

- Local minima → non-convex optimization in deep networks
- Hyperparameter dependence → the tuning "nightmare" in transformers
- Scalability → the central problem of $O(n^2)$ attention
- Slow convergence → the computational cost of autoregressive models

The thesis failed to be practical. But it got one thing right: **energy-based optimization is a fundamental framework for understanding deep learning**. We just needed better energy functions, better nonlinearities, and scalability.
